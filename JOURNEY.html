Chapter 1

An Excel sheet in my inbox

Every big build starts as a small annoyance.

A leader in my org kicked off a World Cup bracket challenge. The format was classic office fun: he emailed out an Excel workbook, everyone filled in their picks — 31 knockout games, a champion, a tiebreaker — and emailed it back. He'd tally the scores as the tournament went on.

I filled mine out like everyone else. But staring at that spreadsheet, I couldn't stop seeing what it could be. All the drama of a World Cup bracket — the upsets, the busted picks, the "my champion is still alive" tension — flattened into cells and columns. The data was begging to be visual.

The first move: design before code

I opened Microsoft Cowork and started designing what I wished existed: a glassmorphism dashboard, KPI cards for points confirmed and points still attainable, and the piece I was proudest of — a bracket map that draws your entire predicted path through the tournament and overlays what actually happened.

The original plan was modest: view my dashboard in Cowork and schedule a sync to pull in new results. That would have been the end of it — a nice personal artifact. It wasn't the end of it.

Instead of keeping it as a prompt, I packaged the whole design as a markdown build kit — a spec anyone could hand to an AI and get the same dashboard with their own picks. Version 6, then version 7, each one tighter. I didn't know the term yet, but I'd stumbled into the idea that would shape everything after: the spec is the real product. The code is just its output.

Chapter 2 · Repo 1 — wc26-bracket

Crossing into GitHub

The project you keep postponing is usually the door to the next level.

Hosting something on GitHub Pages had been on my someday-list for a long time. This was finally the excuse. If my dashboard lived at a URL, I could check it from anywhere, the data could refresh itself, and — this part mattered to me — the entire build process would be preserved in commit history, reverse-engineerable by anyone, including future me.

So I opened GitHub Copilot and we started. What began as one hardcoded HTML file got pulled apart, deliberately, into an actual architecture:

  • The data layer — my picks and the live results extracted into data/*.json, so content and presentation stopped being tangled together
  • The generator — build_dashboard.py, which turns picks + results into the finished dashboard
  • The sync engine — fetch_results.py, pulling finished games from FIFA's public feed
  • The automation — a GitHub Action running the sync three times a day, timed to after each match block, committing new results and redeploying automatically

125 commits in six days. That number isn't a flex — it's what learning in public looks like. Every one of those commits is me figuring out git, workflows, CI, and deployment by actually doing it, with the receipts preserved.

⚙️

The machine that builds itself

Once the sync Action was live, something clicked. I wasn't maintaining a webpage — I'd built a small machine. Games finish, a bot fetches the scores, commits them, redeploys the site. I could close my laptop for the weekend and the dashboard stayed current. That's the difference between making a thing and building a system.

Chapter 3 · Repo 2 — my-wc26-bracket

The first wall: nobody follows instructions

Ship the product, not the instructions.

Proud of the build kit, I shared it. And then I watched what happened: nothing. The person I shared it with was never going to walk through repo setup, Python scripts, and deployment steps to get their own dashboard. Of course they weren't. I wouldn't have either, a month earlier.

That stung for about an hour, and then it reframed the whole problem. The friction wasn't in my instructions — it was in the existence of instructions. The goal had to become: you drop in your Excel bracket, and your dashboard just appears.

What that took

Everything the Python generator did on my machine had to happen in the visitor's browser instead. Copilot and I ported the entire render engine to client-side JavaScript — parsing the workbook with SheetJS, validating it, scoring it against live results, drawing the bracket map. Their spreadsheet never leaves their device.

And because a port this size is exactly where subtle bugs breed, we built a golden test: the JavaScript engine's output is compared byte-for-byte against the original Python's. Not "looks the same" — identical. That test has caught real regressions ever since.

For people without a workbook at all, we added an interactive builder — tap winners round by round, get the same dashboard. The tool stopped being mine and became anyone's.

Chapter 4 · Repo 3 — mywcbracket

The big idea: social, with no backend

The URL is the database.

Individual dashboards were working. But brackets are a social game — the whole point is seeing how you rank against your friends. Every obvious way to build that meant a backend: accounts, a database, hosting bills, and — the real dealbreaker — a central place where my coworkers' real names would live, whether they opted in or not.

The question I kept turning over: could a share link itself carry the bracket — so adding someone to your leaderboard never touches a server at all?

💡

It fits in a link

A bracket is 31 win/lose choices — 31 bits — plus a display name and a tiebreaker. Encoded, that's about 140 characters riding in the URL fragment (the part after #). And by HTTP's own rules, browsers never send the fragment to any server. Not to GitHub, not to anyone. The privacy isn't a policy — it's physics.

Where the workflow leveled up

For this third build I changed how I worked. I'd been reading what the people actually shipping with AI agents do — and the pattern is consistent: they don't prompt, they brief. So before any code, I wrote one. Every brief since has followed the same four-part shape:

G
Goal
The outcome, stated so sharply you'd know if you missed
C
Context
Constraints, history, who it's for, what already exists
S
Source
The ground truth to build from — repos, data, prior art
E
Expectations
Acceptance criteria — what done looks like, verifiably

Then the division of labor that made the weekend possible:

I write the brief→ Fable 5 turns it into staged plans→ I review & adjust the plans→ Cheaper models (Opus 4.8, GPT 5.5) execute the mechanical work→ Fable 5 reviews the output→ I approve each stage

The expensive model does the thinking twice — once planning, once reviewing — and the cheaper models do the typing. My job moved up a level: setting direction, catching drift, and making the judgment calls. Five staged plans, each one shipped and verified before the next began: share links, the add-to-leaderboard loop, the standings and diff view, then polish and hardening.

The Technical Taste Council

One more thing I built for this phase — a judgment framework. I'm not a developer by trade, and my honest gap wasn't syntax, it was taste: knowing when something is quietly over-engineered, badly named, or built for the wrong horizon. So I wrote a council of named voices into the repo's instructions, each owning a domain, and the AI channels them on every non-trivial call:

  • Karpathy — simplest thing that works; no abstraction without a reason it's needed now
  • Hanselman — name things for the next human; every error says what broke and what to do
  • Russinovich — know what's underneath; what breaks under load, bad input, slow networks
  • Willison — verify, don't trust; if a claim can be checked, check it before stating it
  • Naval — does this feature earn its complexity? Prefer decisions that keep future options open
  • Sean Grove — the spec is the artifact; code drifts, intent shouldn't
  • Yegge & Litt — small reviewable increments, in structures I can still modify myself later

Real example of it working: when the "add a rival" feature could have grown a "re-share someone else's bracket" option, the council flagged it — re-broadcasting a coworker's picks without their consent is exactly the leak this design exists to prevent. It's documented in the spec as a decision, not an omission.

Chapter 5

The walls (every single one taught me something)

The bugs you hit yourself are the lessons you keep.

✕
Deploy day

GitHub Pages refused to deploy

The workflow could configure a Pages site but couldn't create one — a permissions subtlety I'd never have guessed. One API call to enable it manually, re-run the workflow, green. Lesson: when automation fails, the gap is usually in what the token is allowed to do, not what the code does.

✕
Mid-session

My own bot pushed against me

The results sync committed new match data while I was working, and my push got rejected. git pull --rebase, re-run the tests, push. Lesson: once you build automation, you're collaborating with it — plan for it.

✕
Testing

My tests would have broken every single day

The golden test read the live results file — which the bot rewrites three times a day. It passed today and was guaranteed to fail tomorrow. Fix: freeze a snapshot of the inputs and test against that. Lesson: a test that depends on moving data isn't a test, it's a countdown.

✕
Data loss

Viewing the demo silently deleted user edits

Opening the demo bracket wiped your what-if score edits — a quiet destroyer that unit tests missed and a browser walkthrough caught. Fix: stash the user's state before any preview, restore it after. Lesson: test the flows a real person actually takes, not just the functions.

✕
Round 2

Eight blank cells in the bracket map

When someone's picks all busted early, the "actual path" view went blank — the code derived reality by walking the user's pick tree, which dead-ends when picks die. Rebuilt it to read each slot straight from real results. We reproduced the worst case first, fixed it, then locked it with five new tests. Lesson: reproduce before you fix, and pin the fix with a test so it can never quietly return.

Chapter 6

The return: back to where it started

You know it worked when the person who sent the spreadsheet asks for the link.

Which brings the story back to the cold open. The same leader who kicked this off with an Excel attachment saw the finished app and asked if he could share it broadly. The circle closed: spreadsheet in, platform out.

What I set out to learn

  • GitHub Copilot, hands-on and in anger
  • Git, commits, CI/CD, and living in the CLI
  • AI architecture: briefs, specs, staged plans
  • Publishing real, public, working software

What I actually learned

  • All of that — plus how to develop taste, deliberately
  • That the brief is where the leverage lives
  • That review is a skill you can get better at fast
  • That "ship the product, not the instructions" applies to almost everything

What the collaboration really looked like

Me
"I'm worried about cost — I want it free. And privacy: these are my coworkers' real names. Could a share link add that user to your dashboard, so we're not compromising anyone who doesn't want to be surfaceable?"
The AI
That constraint pair — free + private — eliminates every backend option and points at one architecture: the URL fragment carries the bracket. It's never sent to a server, so there's nowhere for a name to leak from. Here's a five-stage plan…
Me
"Rebuild the bracket map logic — when my picks bust, the actual path still has to flow through every round. And sometimes the connector lines don't connect."
The AI
Reproduced your worst case: 8 blank cells, and the broken lines are the same bug — blank boxes have no geometry to connect to. Root cause found, fixed, and locked with 5 new tests. Zero blanks now.

Notice what's in my half: outcomes, constraints, and quality bars — not code. That's the job now. The clearer I made the goal, context, sources, and expectations, the better everything downstream got. Every time the output missed, the root cause was in my brief.

And I'm still learning — that's not a disclaimer, it's the design. All three repos are public with full commit history precisely so the process can be inspected, including the missteps. If you look through the code and see something I should have done differently, I genuinely want to hear it. That feedback loop is the whole reason to build in the open.
